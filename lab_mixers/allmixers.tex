\subsection{Fourier Transforms of Noise}

\noindent
Scientifically, noise refers to random fluctuations from a known statistical
distribution (typically Gaussian).
Use the term carefully in your report, 
distinguishing it from systematic errors or bias.

Many astronomical signals appear as noise in the electric field.
We usually measure the power ($P \propto V^2$) of that noise.
Blackbodies emit waves described by the Planck function:
\begin{equation}
B_\nu = \frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/kT}-1}.
\end{equation}
In the Rayleigh-Jeans limit ($h\nu/kT \ll 1$):
\begin{equation}
B_\nu\approx\frac{2kT}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
Noise power depends linearly on temperature $T$.

Radio astronomers express noise power as brightness temperature $T_B$,
defined as $I\equiv2kT_B/\lambda^2$,
even for non-thermal sources like synchrotron emission.

All power sources, including receiver noise, relate to a temperature.
Receiver noise (e.g. Johnson noise from thermal electrons) 
is typically Gaussian (Central Limit Theorem) and characterized by receiver
temperature $T_{rx}$.
This adds to the source temperature $T_B$ to yield the total temperature
as a function of frequency.

\section{Fourier Transforms, Analytic and Discrete (At Home, Week 1)}
\label{pwrspectrum}

\subsection{The Analytic Fourier Transform}

\noindent
A Fourier transform maps a function between complementary coordinates.
The input is usually a time-domain signal $E(t)$; the output is in frequency domain:
%
\begin{equation}
\tilde E(\nu) = \int_{-T/2}^{T/2} E(t) e^{-2 \pi i \nu t} dt \ .
\label{eq:dft}
\end{equation}
%
\noindent
Because of the imaginary exponent, $\tilde E(\nu)$ is complex-valued.
The Fourier Transform is {\bf invertible}:
%
\begin{equation}
E(t) = \frac1{B} \int_{-B/2}^{B/2} \tilde E(\nu) e^{2 \pi i \nu t} d\nu \ .
\end{equation}
%
This works because complex sine waves form a {\bf basis} for the space of functions.
Any function is expressed exactly by its Fourier coefficients.%
\footnote{Analytically, limits $B$ and $T$ are infinite.
In practice, they are bounded, causing spectral leakage.
Note: our forward FT does not divide by the interval, but the inverse does.
This matches {\tt numpy.fft}.}


The Fourier transform is a linear operator (like rotation) with these properties:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Linearity: FT of a sum is the sum of FTs.
\item Invertibility: No information is lost.
\item Unitarity: Power is preserved.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)}
\label{dft}

\noindent
Discrete Fourier transforms differ from analytic ones because sampled signals
are discontinuous.
Integrals become simple sums.
The \verb$ugradio.dft$ module allows manual frequency specification.
It is slow but transparent to understand.
Use it when you need to oversample output frequencies.

Recommendations for \verb$ugradio.dft$:
\begin{itemize}
\item Use even $N$ so the zero frequency falls in a bin center.

\item Center time $t=0$.
For even $N$, run times from $-\frac{N}{2}/ \nu_s$ to $(\frac{N}{2} -1)/ \nu_s$.

\item Calculate output for $N$ frequencies from $-\frac{\nu_s}{2}$ to
$+\frac{\nu_s}{2}\left(1 - \frac{2}{N} \right)$.
This makes $\Delta \nu = \nu_s/N$.
\end{itemize}

Use {\tt ugradio.dft.<tab>} or {\tt ugradio.dft??} to explore the module.

In practice, given the Nyquist criterion and fixed bandwidth of each channel,
algorithmic shortcuts allow DFTs to be computed
extremely efficiently.
\verb$numpy.fft$ is fast but (even over millions of samples), but assumes
equally spaced input time samples and output frequencies.
It also orders output frequencies strangely: 0 to positive max, then negative max to 0.
\verb$numpy.fft.ifft$ requires this order as input.

The function \verb$fftshift$ reorders frequencies for plotting (negative to positive).
\textbf{Only use {\tt fftshift} for plotting.}
It breaks \verb$ifft$.
Use \verb$fftfreq$ to calculate output frequencies.


\subsection{Power Spectra and Discrete Fourier Transforms}
\label{pwrspect}

\noindent
The {\bf power spectrum} $P_\nu$ is proportional to voltage squared.
For complex quantities, multiply the voltage by its conjugate:
%
\begin{equation}
P_\nu = \tilde E(\nu) \tilde E^*(\nu) \ .
\end{equation}
%
\noindent In Python: {\tt P = E * E.conj()}.
The imaginary part should be zero.
If not, cast as float to remove rounding errors.

Alternatively, use \verb$P = numpy.abs(E)**2$.
This automatically returns a real result.


\subsection{The Power Spectrum and the Autocorrelation Function (ACF)}
\label{acf}

\noindent
Radio astronomy requires facility with the {\bf convolution} and
{\bf correlation} theorems.

Convolution of $E$ and $F$:
%
\begin{equation}
[E * F](\tau) = \int_{-T/2}^{+T/2} E(t) F(\tau - t) \ dt,
\end{equation}
%
Correlation:
%
\begin{equation}
[E\star F](\tau) = \int_{-T/2}^{+T/2} E(t) F(\tau + t) \ dt.
\end{equation}
%
\noindent $\tau$ represents the time shift (delay) between functions.
Convolution flips the sign of $t$ in $F$; correlation does not.

The Convolution Theorem:
%
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{[E*F]}(\nu) = \tilde E(\nu) \cdot \tilde F(\nu)
\end{equation}
%
The Correlation Theorem:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{[E\star F]}(\nu) = \tilde E(\nu) \cdot \tilde F^*(\nu)
\label{eq:acfpwr}
\end{equation}
%
The FT of the convolution/correlation is the product of the FTs.

When $E(t)=F(t)$, correlation becomes the {\bf Autocorrelation function} (ACF).
Equation \ref{eq:acfpwr} states the power spectrum is the Fourier transform of the ACF.

These theorems assume $T \rightarrow \infty$.
Digital ACFs suffer from end effects.
For $N$ samples with delay $\Delta N$, the number of summed terms decreases as
$\Delta N$ increases.

\section{On Mixers and the Heterodyne Process (At Home, Week 2)}
\label{hetereo}

\subsection{The Heterodyne Process}
%
\noindent
Mixers shift the entire input spectrum by a uniform amount.
They multiply the signal by a Local Oscillator (LO) sine wave ($\nu_{LO}$).

This allows antennas to tune to incoming radiation while filters and samplers
operate at lower fixed frequencies.
AM radios, for example, shift MHz signals to audio (kHz) frequencies.
Tuning a station changes the LO frequency; the chain remains static.

This is a {\bf heterodyne} receiver.

\subsection{Single Sideband (SSB) Mixer Theory}

\noindent
SSB theory is best understood with complex numbers and {\bf negative frequencies}.
Euler's formula defines a complex sinusoid:
\begin{equation}
Ae^{i\omega t} = A\cos(\omega t) + i\cdot A\sin(\omega t).
\end{equation}
Negative frequencies might seem weird.
How can something oscillate a negative number of times per second?
Negating frequency swaps the sign of the imaginary component.
Negative frequency implies a phase relationship between sine and cosine.

Consider an idealized SSB mixer with LO $e^{-i\wLO t}$.
Input signal:
\begin{equation}
E(t)=A\sin (\wLO-\Delta\omega)t + B \sin (\wLO+\Delta\omega)t.
\end{equation}
Mixing multiplies $E(t)$ by the LO.
Using Euler's formula, the product contains beat frequencies $e^{\pm i \Delta\omega t}$
and high frequency terms ($2\wLO$).
Low-pass filtering removes the high frequencies:
\begin{equation}
{\rm LPF}\left[E(t)\cdot e^{-i\wLO t}\right] =
  \frac{A}{2i}e^{-i\Delta\omega t} + \frac{B}{2i} e^{i\Delta\omega t}.
\end{equation}
The signals shift to lower frequencies.
Retaining real and imaginary parts preserves the distinction between $A$ and $B$.
Positive and negative {\bf sidebands} remain separate.

Figure \ref{ssb} shows the SSB mixer.
The left mixer is DSB.
The right mixer uses a $90^\circ$ delayed LO.
Its output (Im(IF)) becomes the sine component to the left's cosine (Re(IF)).


\subsection{Double Sideband (DSB) Mixer Theory}

\noindent
DSB mixers are simpler to build but harder to interpret.
Using LO $\sin \wLO t$ and signal $E(t)=A\sin(\wLO+\Delta\omega)t$:
\begin{align}
E(t)\cdot \sin\wLO t &= A\left[\cos\Delta\omega t - \cos(2\wLO +\Delta)t\right].
\end{align}
Filtering yields a beat frequency.

However, if $E(t)=A\sin(\wLO +\Delta\omega)t + B\sin(\wLO -\Delta\omega)t$:
\begin{equation}
{\rm LPF}\left[E(t)\cdot \sin\wLO t\right] = (A+B)\cos\Delta\omega t.
\end{equation}
Both $\wLO +\Delta\omega$ and $\wLO -\Delta\omega$ map to the same output frequency.
The DSB mixer cannot distinguish positive and negative deviations.
It stacks sidebands on top of each other.

DSB mixing creates symmetric spectra for USB and LSB.
Without filters, they sum inextricably.
